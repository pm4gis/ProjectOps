\# What is ProjectOps



ProjectOps is an operating model for delivering change from idea to measurable outcome. It integrates planning, requirements, delivery, business change, and benefits into one system, so teams do not lose intent, context, or momentum across hand-offs.



ProjectOps is not a new methodology you must follow strictly. It is a practical structure that helps you run work with clear ownership, traceable decisions, and a steady flow of delivery and adoption.



---



\## The problem ProjectOps solves



Most delivery environments split work into disconnected streams:



\- Planning and funding decisions are made without enough delivery input.

\- Requirements are captured, then drift as delivery progresses.

\- Delivery finishes, then change management starts.

\- Benefits are written down early, then forgotten.

\- Decisions sit in meetings, emails, and chats, and are hard to audit later.



These gaps create rework, delays, and disagreement about what was meant to happen.



ProjectOps reduces those gaps by keeping a connected set of artefacts and routines that travel with the work.



---



\## The definition



ProjectOps is:



\- an integrated workflow from idea to outcome

\- a consistent set of lightweight artefacts

\- a small set of routines that maintain alignment

\- a way to track outcomes, not just tasks



ProjectOps can sit across many tools. It is tool-agnostic.



---



\## ProjectOps in one view



\*\*Idea → Plan → Deliver → Adopt → Measure → Learn\*\*



Each phase has a clear purpose and produces evidence you can use later.



\- Idea: why this matters and what outcome is desired

\- Plan: what will be done, in what order, and what it depends on

\- Deliver: build or implement changes with quality and visibility

\- Adopt: ensure people can use the change, with readiness and support

\- Measure: confirm outcomes and benefits after release

\- Learn: capture what worked and what should change next time



---



\## What ProjectOps includes



\### 1) Clear outcome definition



Every piece of work needs a clear statement of:



\- the outcome you want

\- how it will be measured

\- who owns the outcome



A useful test is: could you tell, later, if it worked.



---



\### 2) Traceable requirements and decisions



ProjectOps treats decisions and requirements as operational assets:



\- decisions are logged with date, owner, reason, and impacts

\- requirements are small, testable, and linked to outcomes

\- changes to scope are recorded so there is one version of the truth



This reduces debate and helps governance without heavy paperwork.



---



\### 3) Integrated planning and delivery



Planning is not a separate activity that ends when delivery begins.



ProjectOps keeps planning connected to execution by maintaining:



\- visible dependencies

\- a realistic sequence (milestones, iterations, or both)

\- a clear definition of done for each item

\- early surfacing of risks and constraints



---



\### 4) Built-in business change and adoption



ProjectOps assumes that delivery is incomplete until the change is usable.



It includes:



\- stakeholder mapping

\- change impact assessment

\- communications and training planned alongside delivery

\- readiness checks before release

\- post-release support and adoption review



---



\### 5) Benefits and outcomes tracking after delivery



ProjectOps treats benefits as something you manage, not something you declare.



It includes:



\- baseline measures

\- benefit owners

\- review points (for example 30, 60, 90 days after release)

\- evidence capture so you can report with confidence



---



\## The five practices



ProjectOps can be run through five connected practices. You can adopt them gradually.



\- \*\*PlanningOps\*\*: prioritisation, sequencing, dependencies, scope boundaries

\- \*\*BuildOps\*\*: delivery flow, quality, release readiness, risk and issue control

\- \*\*ChangeOps\*\*: stakeholder engagement, readiness, training, communications, support

\- \*\*ValueOps\*\*: measures, benefits, outcome checks, value reporting

\- \*\*KnowledgeOps\*\*: decisions, requirements, lessons learned, audit-friendly history



---



\## Roles and ownership



ProjectOps works best when ownership is explicit.



Typical ownership patterns:



\- outcome owner: accountable for benefits and success measures

\- delivery lead: accountable for delivery flow and release readiness

\- change lead: accountable for readiness, comms, training, support

\- product or service owner: accountable for long-term operational health

\- governance: accountable for prioritisation, risk appetite, and scope decisions



One person can cover multiple roles in small teams.



---



\## What success looks like



ProjectOps is working when you see:



\- faster agreement on priorities and scope

\- fewer last-minute surprises before go-live

\- fewer disputes about what was decided and why

\- adoption increasing after release, with support issues decreasing

\- benefits measured and reported with evidence

\- easier audits and reviews because information is findable



---



\## Where to start



Start small, then expand.



A simple first implementation:



1\. Create a one-page project brief (outcome, scope, measures, owners).

2\. Keep a decision log from day one.

3\. Keep a single RAID log with owners and review cadence.

4\. Add a readiness checklist before each release.

5\. Schedule one outcome check after release and record results.



That alone improves alignment and reduces rework.
